# The First Italo-Ethiopian War and the Battle of Adwa

## Relationship between the war and the battle

The Battle of Adwa was not a separate conflict from the war with Italy. It was
the decisive battle of the Italo-Ethiopian War, fought between the
Ethiopian Empire and the Kingdom of Italy from 1895 to 1896. The battle took
place on 1 March 1896 and effectively ended the war. Ethiopia won, and Italy
abandoned its attempt to make the country a protectorate.

The war is sometimes called the First Italo-Abyssinian War. The Second
Italo-Ethiopian War is a different conflict, fought in 1935-1936 under Mussolini,
four decades later.

## Where the battle was fought

The battle was fought near the town of Adwa in the Tigray region of northern
Ethiopia. The surrounding country is mountainous and broken, with steep ridges
and narrow valleys. The terrain separated the advancing Italian columns from one
another and left them unable to support each other, which shaped the outcome.

## Cause of the war: the Treaty of Wuchale

The war grew out of the Treaty of Wuchale, signed on 2 May 1889 between Menelik
II and Italy. The treaty was written in two languages, and Article XVII differed
between them.

* The Amharic text said Ethiopia *could* make use of Italy in its dealings with
  other foreign powers, leaving the choice with Ethiopia.
* The Italian text said Ethiopia *must* conduct its foreign affairs through
  Italy, which would make Ethiopia an Italian protectorate.

Italy relied on its own version to claim a protectorate over Ethiopia. Menelik II
rejected that reading and denounced the treaty in 1893. The dispute over this
single article is the immediate cause of the war. The treaty name is also spelled
Wichale, Wuchale, or Ucciale.

## Who fought

On the Ethiopian side, Emperor Menelik II led the army and Empress Taytu Betul
took an active part in both the campaign and the diplomacy. Senior commanders
included Ras Alula Engida, Ras Makonnen Wolde Mikael, Ras Mengesha Yohannes, Ras
Mikael of Wollo, and Fitawrari Gebeyehu. Menelik drew forces from across the
Ethiopian regions, which gave him a far larger army than the Italians expected.

On the Italian side, General Oreste Baratieri commanded the forces in Eritrea.
His army advanced in separate brigades under Matteo Albertone, Vittorio
Dabormida, Giuseppe Arimondi, and Giuseppe Ellena, supported by Eritrean colonial
troops known as ascari.

## Events leading to the battle

* Battle of Coatit, January 1895: an early engagement between Italian forces and
  Ras Mengesha.
* Battle of Amba Alagi, December 1895: Ethiopian forces defeated an Italian
  detachment under Pietro Toselli.
* Siege of Mekelle, January 1896: Ethiopian forces besieged the Italian fort.
  Empress Taytu Betul advised cutting off the garrison's water supply, and the
  defenders surrendered.

These actions pushed Baratieri into a difficult position, under political
pressure from Rome to attack rather than withdraw.

## The battle and its outcome

Baratieri ordered a night march toward the Ethiopian positions, intending to
surprise them. The columns became separated in the dark over unfamiliar and
difficult ground, and by morning they were engaged piecemeal rather than as a
single force. Ethiopian forces defeated each in turn. The fighting was over
within a day and ended in a decisive Ethiopian victory.

Italian losses were heavy, and a substantial number of Italian soldiers were
taken prisoner. Published casualty estimates differ considerably between sources,
so precise figures should be treated with caution.

## Aftermath

The Treaty of Addis Ababa, signed in October 1896, ended the war. It annulled the
Treaty of Wuchale and Italy recognised the full independence and sovereignty of
Ethiopia. Italy retained the colony of Eritrea. Baratieri was recalled and tried,
and the Italian government of Francesco Crispi fell as a result of the defeat.

## Why the battle matters

Adwa preserved Ethiopian independence during the period when the rest of the
continent was being partitioned among European powers, and it forced a European
state to abandon a colonial claim by treaty. It became a reference point for
anti-colonial movements and for pan-African thought well beyond Ethiopia. The
victory is commemorated in Ethiopia as Adwa Victory Day, observed on Yekatit 23
in the Ethiopian calendar, which falls on 2 March.

Adwa is often described as the first African victory over a European colonial
power. That description is not accurate. African forces had defeated European
armies before, including the Zulu victory over the British at Isandlwana in 1879
and the Ethiopian defeat of an Italian force at Dogali in 1887. What sets Adwa
apart is the scale and the consequence: it ended an entire colonial campaign,
and Italy recognised Ethiopian sovereignty by treaty as a result.

Adwa was also not the first battle of the First Italo-Ethiopian War. Coatit, Amba
Alagi and the siege of Mekelle came before it. Adwa was the final and decisive
battle of that war.
